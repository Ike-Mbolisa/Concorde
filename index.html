Background

What Was the Concorde?

The Concorde was a joint British–French engineering project, the product of an international treaty signed in 1962. It entered commercial service in 1976, operated by British Airways and Air France, and flew for 27 years before its retirement in 2003.

Flying at altitudes above 18,300 metres — higher than most weather — passengers could see the curvature of the Earth. The aircraft's aluminium skin reached 127 °C during cruise, causing the fuselage to expand by up to 25 cm. Windows were warm to the touch.

The delta wing design, with no separate horizontal tailplane, was chosen because it produces low drag at supersonic speeds. The distinctive drooped nose allowed pilots to see the runway during the steep approach that the design demanded.

Engines

4 × Rolls-Royce/SNECMA Olympus 593 turbojets, each producing 169.3 kN of thrust with reheat. The engines consumed fuel at roughly 25,629 kg/h at cruise.

Materials Challenge

Conventional aluminium alloys soften at ~150 °C. Concorde used a special RR.58 aluminium-copper alloy that retains strength at operating temperatures.

Sonic Boom

Flying supersonically over land was banned in most countries. Concorde was restricted to oceanic routes because its sonic boom — roughly 100 dB on the ground — would disturb populations below.

Economic Fate

The 2000 Air France crash, rising fuel costs after 9/11, and a declining passenger market led both airlines to retire the fleet in 2003. Only 20 aircraft were ever built.

History

Key Milestones

1962

International Treaty

Britain and France sign the treaty mandating joint development of a supersonic transport aircraft.

1969

First Flight

Concorde 001 makes its maiden flight from Toulouse on 2 March 1969. It breaks the sound barrier two months later.

1976

Commercial Service Begins

British Airways and Air France launch simultaneous commercial services — London to Bahrain, and Paris to Rio de Janeiro.

1996

Speed Record

Concorde sets the transatlantic crossing record — New York to London in 2 hours, 52 minutes, 59 seconds.

2000

Air France Flight 4590

A runway debris strike causes a tyre blowout and fuel tank rupture. All 113 aboard die. The fleet is grounded for 15 months.

2003

Final Flight

Concorde makes its last commercial flight on 24 October 2003. Rising costs and falling demand ended the supersonic era.
